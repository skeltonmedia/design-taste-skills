# Evidence rules

What may enter the DNA, how it is marked, and who moves it.

## The bar

A line enters the DNA only with one of these behind it:

1. **A decision.** The subject chose A over B on a date. Goes to section 2 with the date.
2. **An artifact.** Something shipped or declined that can be opened and looked at. Goes to section 2 or 3 with the date or the rejection entry.
3. **An interview answer.** The subject said it, in a logged session. Goes to the library row first; it reaches the DNA through the tally.

Anything else is an inference. Inferences are allowed, marked `[inferred]`, and listed in the lint report until the subject confirms or strikes them.

## The three-appearance rule

A signal from the library becomes a proposal when its key appears in rows from three separate session dates. One appearance is a data point. Two is a coincidence. Three is taste. The proposal cites the three dates.

A rejection does not wait for three. One clear no with a stated reason is a section 3 line, because rejections are the sharpest signal and the costliest to repeat.

## Marks

- `[inferred]`: an agent read it from a pattern; the subject has not said it.
- `[aspiration]`: the subject wants it and cannot yet name its cost. Stays in the library, never in sections 0 to 5.
- `~~struck~~` with a date: a line the subject removed. Struck lines stay visible. The file records movement; it does not hide it.

## Conflicts

New evidence that contradicts a recorded line is never a silent edit. It becomes a section 6 question with both sources. The subject rules; the ruling and the date stay in section 6.

## What is never evidence

- Trends, award lists, "best practice", or another subject's DNA.
- The agent's own preference, however well argued.
- A single review's verdicts. `apply` cites the DNA; it does not write it.
